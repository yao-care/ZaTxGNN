## Phenobarbital: Trigeminal nerve neoplasm

## One-Sentence Summary

Phenobarbital is a long-established barbiturate anticonvulsant, originally used for epilepsy (including partial and generalised tonic-clonic seizures) and a range of headache and migraine conditions.
The TxGNN model's top-ranked prediction is **trigeminal nerve neoplasm**, but there are **no registered clinical trials** and only **1 publication** (a case series on Sturge-Weber syndrome), which does not support an anti-tumour role.
The more plausible predicted indications are reflex-type seizure disorders, which are extensions of phenobarbital's existing epilepsy use, although direct evidence for them is also very limited.

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Currently, detailed mechanism of action data is not available in the data compiled for this report. Phenobarbital is generally understood to enhance GABA-A receptor-mediated inhibition, which underlies its established use in focal and generalised epilepsy.

**Trigeminal nerve neoplasm (rank 1):** There is no clear mechanistic link. The only retrieved paper is a case series on Sturge-Weber syndrome, a neurocutaneous disorder in which phenobarbital is used to control seizures, not to treat tumours. The high score probably reflects network proximity to epilepsy and neurological terms rather than anti-tumour activity. This prediction should not be pursued as a cancer treatment.

Because phenobarbital already treats epilepsy, GABAergic enhancement is a plausible fit. However, the retrieved literature is mostly general or neonatal seizure guidance, preclinical animal studies, and isolated case reports. None demonstrates phenobarbital efficacy for these specific conditions. No literature at all was retrieved for orgasm-induced seizures.

**Trigeminal neuralgia (rank 9):** Carbamazepine is the first-line anticonvulsant, with baclofen, phenytoin and valproate also effective. Phenobarbital is not an established treatment. An older cat study suggested phenobarbital tends to depress, rather than facilitate, trigeminal inhibitory mechanisms, and it is relevant mainly as an enzyme inducer that lowers carbamazepine levels.

**Beta-ketothiolase deficiency (rank 10):** This is an inborn error of ketone and isoleucine metabolism. No mechanistic basis for benefit is evident, and no literature was found.

## Safety Considerations

**Contraindications flagged in the knowledge graph** (these come from drug-disease relationship data, not from approved label text):

- Hepatic: liver failure, liver disease, gallbladder disease
- Renal: kidney disease, pyoureter
- Respiratory: chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (including severe early-onset COPD)
- Metabolic/endocrine: inherited porphyria, porphyria, porphyrin metabolism disease, hyperglycaemia, hypothyroidism, myxedema, congenital hypothyroidism
- Cardiovascular and haematological: hypertension (hypertensive disorder), thrombocytopenia
- Gastrointestinal: gastric ulcer, peptic ulcer disease, gastritis
- Psychiatric: insomnia, anxiety disorder, neurotic disorder, dysthymic disorder, psychotic disorder, schizophreniform disorder, schizoaffective disorder, mental disorder
- Autoimmune: systemic lupus erythematosus (including paediatric)

**Drug interactions** (100 interactions recorded; examples from the first 20 listed):

- **Major:** morphine (including liposomal morphine), eliglustat, naldemedine, naloxegol, rolapitant
- **Moderate:** doxycycline, metronidazole, bupropion, pioglitazone, aprepitant, dronabinol, cholecalciferol, pyridoxine, and the corticosteroids (hydrocortisone, prednisolone, prednisone, dexamethasone, betamethasone, triamcinolone)

Phenobarbital is a strong enzyme inducer, which explains many of these interactions. The full list should be checked before any use.

**Other points from the literature:** neonatal exposure raised concern about neuronal apoptosis and long-term cognitive effects in animal studies, and early treatment of febrile seizures was associated with lowered IQ in children. These are relevant to any expansion of use in young patients.

Please refer to the SAHPRA-approved Professional Information (PI) for official warnings and contraindications, as these were not available for this report. Report adverse drug reactions to SAHPRA.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold**

**Rationale:**
- The top prediction (trigeminal nerve neoplasm) has no trial or literature support and no plausible anti-tumour mechanism. The score appears to reflect network proximity to epilepsy rather than real activity.
- The reflex seizure predictions are logical extensions of phenobarbital's existing epilepsy use, but evidence is indirect (general seizure or neonatal guidance, animal models, isolated case reports). Thinking seizures and micturition-induced seizures are worth flagging as research questions, not as ready for adoption.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Download and review the SAHPRA package insert, as the warnings and contraindications are a blocking data gap for safety screening
- Obtain mechanism of action data from DrugBank
- Confirm the approved indications for the registered product (Propain forte) and its EML status, noting it may be a combination-type brand despite being listed as single-ingredient
- For the reflex epilepsies, targeted literature review and case series or registry data on phenobarbital use in these conditions
- Deprioritise trigeminal nerve neoplasm and beta-ketothiolase deficiency unless new mechanistic evidence emerges
