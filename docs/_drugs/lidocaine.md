# Lidocaine: From Dermatitis and Neuralgia to Punctate Epithelial Keratoconjunctivitis

## One-Sentence Summary

Lidocaine is a local anaesthetic and sodium channel blocker, originally used for conditions such as dermatitis, neuralgia, haemorrhoids and postherpetic neuralgia.
The TxGNN model predicts it may be effective for **punctate epithelial keratoconjunctivitis**, but **no clinical trials and no publications** currently support this specific prediction.
Evidence for the other predicted ocular indications is also thin. The best-supported is **conjunctival disorder** (2 trials, 20 publications), and that evidence concerns procedural anaesthesia rather than disease treatment.

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Detailed mechanism of action data is not available in the data compiled for this report. Lidocaine is generally known as a sodium channel blocker that suppresses nerve impulse conduction, and it is used as a topical, injectable and ocular surface anaesthetic.

Its original indications (neuralgia, postherpetic neuralgia, dermatitis-related itch and pain) are mostly symptom-relief uses based on sensory nerve blockade. The same mechanism could relieve discomfort in ocular surface conditions such as punctate epithelial keratoconjunctivitis.

The link is limited to symptom control. Lidocaine has no known disease-modifying effect on epithelial inflammation. Prolonged topical anaesthetic use is also known to be toxic to the corneal epithelium, so the prediction should be treated as a hypothesis only.

Other predicted indications have similarly weak rationales:
- **Atopic conjunctivitis (L4):** a 2023 preclinical study (PMID 37819721) describes a nerve–goblet cell association that promotes allergen passage, which hints that sensory nerve blockade might modulate allergic responses. The evidence is indirect.
- **Conjunctival disorder (L4):** lidocaine is used for anaesthesia in conjunctival procedures such as pterygium excision, which is procedural analgesia rather than disease treatment.
- **Papillary conjunctivitis, rosacea conjunctivitis, tinea corporis, nephrotic syndrome and non-human animal disease:** no plausible disease-modifying mechanism was identified.

## Safety Considerations

**Contraindications (knowledge graph signals, not label text):** The TxGNN knowledge graph links lidocaine to contraindication edges for the following disease groups, which require verification against the SAHPRA-approved Professional Information:
- Cardiovascular: hypertensive disorder, hypertension
- Hepatic and renal: liver failure, liver disease, cirrhosis of liver, kidney disease, gallbladder disease, pyoureter
- Neurological and psychiatric: epilepsy, psychotic disorder, schizophreniform disorder, schizoaffective disorder, mental disorder
- Endocrine and metabolic: adrenocortical insufficiency, Addison disease, hypothyroidism, myxoedema, congenital hypothyroidism, osteoporosis, postmenopausal osteoporosis, potassium deficiency disease
- Infectious and inflammatory: measles, varicella zoster infection, herpes simplex virus keratitis, fungal infectious disease, dermatophytosis, atopic eczema, atopic dermatitis, gastritis
- Ocular: open-angle glaucoma

**Drug Interactions:** The interaction screen returned 100 interacting drugs. The first 20 entries reviewed are listed below.
- **Major:** bupropion
- **Moderate:** aprepitant, cimetidine, liposomal morphine, obeticholic acid, polyethylene glycol (3350 with electrolytes), picosulfuric acid, sodium sulfate
- **Minor:** dexamethasone, kanamycin, neomycin, omeprazole, streptomycin, troglitazone
- **Unknown severity:** calcitriol, glimepiride, doxycycline, clotrimazole, pantoprazole, mesalazine

**Safety signals in the literature:**
- Neuropsychiatric and cardiac adverse effects with systemic lidocaine (PMID 19250287)
- Methaemoglobinaemia with topical lidocaine combined with other methaemoglobin-inducing drugs (PMID 41995267)
- Seizures at low lidocaine doses in an animal report (PMID 19858165)
- Prolonged topical anaesthetic use is known to be toxic to the corneal epithelium. Topical anaesthetic abuse is a recognised cause of keratitis, which argues against use in keratitis conditions.

Please refer to the SAHPRA-approved Professional Information (PI) for label warnings and contraindications. Report adverse drug reactions to SAHPRA.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold**

**Rationale:**
The top predictions are model output only (L5), with no trials or literature for punctate epithelial keratoconjunctivitis. The best-supported ocular signal (conjunctival disorder, L4) reflects procedural anaesthesia rather than disease treatment. Known corneal epithelial toxicity with prolonged topical use is a safety concern for the predicted ocular conditions. Atopic conjunctivitis may merit follow-up as a research question only.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Mechanism of action data to support a mechanistic-link analysis
- SAHPRA Professional Information warnings and contraindications for a safety screen
- Confirmation of an ophthalmic lidocaine formulation and route in South Africa, since the two current registrations are an injection and an oral tablet
- Controlled clinical data in the predicted ocular conditions that measure disease outcomes, not procedural anaesthesia, with corneal safety assessment
- Mechanistic studies of sensory nerve blockade in allergic conjunctivitis, if that research question is pursued
