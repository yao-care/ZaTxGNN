# Ketoconazole: From Superficial and Systemic Fungal Infections to Acne

## One-Sentence Summary

Ketoconazole is an azole antifungal originally used for fungal skin infections (such as tinea, seborrhoeic dermatitis and candidiasis) and some systemic mycoses.
The TxGNN model predicts it may be effective for **acne**, but the evidence is still early: **1 registered clinical trial** (small, still in progress, topical use) and a handful of laboratory and review publications.
This is best treated as a research question, not a treatment recommendation.

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Currently, detailed mechanism of action data is not available in the data compiled for this report. Based on general pharmacology, ketoconazole is an azole antifungal that blocks fungal ergosterol synthesis, and its efficacy in fungal skin disease is well established.

Several plausible links to acne exist:
- **Yeast involvement:** Malassezia (Pityrosporum) yeasts are linked to folliculitis and acne-like eruptions, including neonatal Malassezia pustulosis. Some "acne" is in fact Malassezia folliculitis, which ketoconazole would be expected to treat.
- **Anti-bacterial effects:** Laboratory studies report that azoles, including ketoconazole, inhibit the growth and lipase activity of *Propionibacterium acnes* (now *Cutibacterium acnes*), which is relevant given rising antibiotic resistance.
- **Androgen effects:** At higher systemic doses, ketoconazole inhibits steroid-synthesis enzymes (such as CYP17A1) and lowers androgen production, which could matter for hormone-driven acne, e.g. in PCOS.
- **Anti-inflammatory activity:** Some anti-inflammatory activity has also been described.

The very high TxGNN score is a computational prediction and is not clinical evidence. Systemic use for acne is limited by serious safety concerns (see Safety Considerations), so any realistic path would be topical.

## Safety Considerations

- **Contraindications and disease associations (from the knowledge graph, not label text):** long QT syndrome (including familial), torsades de pointes, liver disease, gallbladder disease, adrenocortical insufficiency/Addison disease, skin atrophy, telangiectasia and related capillary disease, peripheral vascular and arterial disease, and achlorhydria. Several of these relate mainly to systemic use.
- **Systemic-use concerns:** Serious liver toxicity, adrenal suppression and QT prolongation limit systemic use for a non-life-threatening condition such as acne.
- **Drug interactions:** 100 interactions were recorded. Examples include:
  - **Major:** loperamide, triamcinolone, budesonide.
  - **Moderate:** famotidine, ranitidine, omeprazole, rabeprazole (acid-suppressing drugs that can reduce absorption of oral ketoconazole), hydrocortisone, dexamethasone, betamethasone, pioglitazone, aprepitant, alosetron, and several anticholinergics (atropine, hyoscyamine, methscopolamine).
  - **Minor:** amphotericin B.

SAHPRA-approved Professional Information (PI) warnings were not available in the data compiled; please consult the current PI before any use. Report adverse drug reactions to SAHPRA.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold**

**Rationale:**
The only supporting clinical study is a small, unfinished, topical trial with no results, and the rest of the evidence is laboratory data, reviews and case reports. The high TxGNN score is not clinical evidence, and systemic use carries significant safety risks.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Results from NCT07237763 and, ideally, larger randomised trials of topical ketoconazole in acne, including Malassezia-positive versus Malassezia-negative patients
- Mechanism of action data and the SAHPRA PI warnings and contraindications
- Confirmation of the approved indications and formulations of the two SAHPRA-registered products, and EML status
- A clear decision on route: topical use only, given the systemic safety concerns and the large interaction burden
