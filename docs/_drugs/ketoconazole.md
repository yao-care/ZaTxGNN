# Ketoconazole: From Fungal Infections to Acne

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Currently, detailed mechanism of action data is not available in this evidence pack. Based on known pharmacology, ketoconazole is an imidazole antifungal that inhibits fungal cytochrome P450 (lanosterol 14α-demethylase). Separately from its antifungal activity, several of the identified publications describe an off-target effect against *Propionibacterium (Cutibacterium) acnes* — the bacterium implicated in acne pathogenesis — including direct inhibition of *P. acnes* lipase and demonstrable in vitro antibacterial activity, which offers a plausible mechanistic bridge to acne.

Ketoconazole's original use covers fungal and yeast-related dermatological conditions (e.g., dermatophytosis, seborrheic dermatitis, *Malassezia*-associated disease). Acne and seborrheic dermatitis share overlapping pathophysiology — both involve the sebaceous/follicular unit, sebum metabolism, and local microbial-driven inflammation — which supports biological plausibility for repurposing a topical antifungal already used in adjacent skin conditions.

The strongest mechanistic support comes from two in vitro studies (PMID 28111792, PMID 20045949) showing azole antifungals, including ketoconazole, directly inhibit *P. acnes* lipase and bacterial growth — the same lipase activity thought to drive comedonal and inflammatory acne lesions. This has now progressed to a randomized, active (not yet completed) trial comparing topical ketoconazole 2% cream against topical adapalene 2% in mild acne (NCT07237763), representing an early translational step from mechanism to clinical testing.
