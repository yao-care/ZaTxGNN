## Clioquinol: From Tinea Infections to Cutaneous Candidiasis
{: .text-alpha }

## One-Sentence Summary

Clioquinol is a topical halogenated hydroxyquinoline antifungal, originally used for tinea pedis, tinea corporis and related dermatophyte infections.
The TxGNN model predicts it may be effective for **cutaneous candidiasis**,
but the evidence is very thin: **0 clinical trials** and **1 indirect publication** (a 1988 in vitro study on soap additives).

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Detailed mechanism of action data is not available in the data compiled for this report. Clioquinol is a halogenated hydroxyquinoline, a class known for metal-chelating and antifungal activity. Its efficacy against dermatophyte skin infections is the basis of its original indications.

Candida species are plausibly susceptible to the same antifungal activity. Cutaneous candidiasis and tinea are both superficial fungal skin infections, and topical treatment is the usual route for both. Extension to cutaneous candidiasis is therefore biologically reasonable.

However, the only literature hit is a 1988 occupational-prevention article. It is indirect and does not show clinical efficacy. The very high TxGNN score is a model prediction, not clinical evidence.

## Clinical Trial Evidence

It is preclinical, but it supports clioquinol's antifungal activity and flags the need to attend to toxicity.

## Safety Considerations

**Contraindication signals from the knowledge graph** (these come from drug-disease relationship data, not from the product label):
- Adrenocortical insufficiency and Addison disease
- Skin atrophy
- Telangiectasis, capillary disease and hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia
- Peripheral vascular disease, intermittent claudication and peripheral arterial disease

These signals likely reflect the corticosteroid components of the combination products rather than clioquinol alone, and should be checked against the product labels.

**Other considerations:**
- The 2021 *Mycoses* study notes that toxicity and irritant potential need attention when clioquinol is combined with other antifungals.
- No drug interaction records were found.

Please refer to the SAHPRA-approved Professional Information (PI) for warnings and contraindications, which were not available in the compiled data. Report adverse drug reactions to SAHPRA.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold**

**Rationale:**
The prediction is biologically plausible, but there are no clinical trials and the only publication is a 1988 indirect in vitro article. The model score alone is not enough to support a repurposing decision. Safety review also cannot proceed, because the label warnings and contraindications are missing.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- SAHPRA package insert warnings and contraindications (a blocking gap for safety screening)
- Mechanism of action data (for example from DrugBank)
- Approved indication text for the two registered products, and confirmation of whether clioquinol is the active ingredient in each (both are combination-type topical products)
- Direct evidence of clioquinol activity against *Candida* species, such as in vitro susceptibility data and then clinical studies in cutaneous candidiasis
- A comparison against established topical antifungals already used for cutaneous candidiasis

**Other predicted indications (all Hold, except superficial mycosis):**
- Most of the other predictions have no trials or literature (L5).
- Superficial mycosis (score 99.18%, L4) has only the 2021 preclinical combination study and is classed as a Research Question.
- Tinea manuum (94.12%) is essentially an extension of the existing tinea indications rather than true repurposing.
- Deep or hair-follicle dermatophyte infections (Majocchi granuloma, ectothrix, endothrix, tinea profunda, scalp or beard dermatophytosis) are poor fits for a topical agent, since they usually require systemic antifungals.
- Granuloma annulare (95.27%) is a non-infectious inflammatory disease with no clear mechanistic rationale.
